# Level risk: the half of a residual value that no car-level model can see

Generated by `analysis/level_risk.py` from `data/reference/price_indices.parquet`. No new data source; this is the reference table `build_reference.py` already builds.

A three-year lease fixes a residual value today and learns in 36 months whether it was right. Two things can make it wrong: **the curve** - how fast this particular car lost value - and **the level** - what the whole used market was worth on the day it came back. Every other analysis in this project measures the curve. This one measures the level, in the same unit, so the two can be put side by side.

## Method

Coverage is very uneven. France and Lithuania run from 1996; most markets begin between 2014 and 2016. Pooling every overlapping window across all markets would let those two supply a third of the sample, so the headline panel uses a **common window, 2016-12 to 2025-12**, in which every market contributes the same number of windows. 26 EU national markets clear that bar. Dropped for short coverage: BE. Aggregates (EA, EU27) are excluded because they would double-count their members. Long histories are reported separately below as a check.

## Curve error against level error, in the same unit

A car that keeps 75.0% of its list price after 3 years at the pooled 9.2%-a-year rate is the reference point. Each row below asks how far that number moves when one source of error is applied.

| source of error | measured range | where from | points of list price at 3y |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Curve**, in a market with its own data | -13.2% to -11.7% a year | `latvia_time_report.md`, 52 monthly refits | 3.5 |
| **Curve**, in a market with no history of its own | -13.1% to -5.0% a year | `drivers_report.md`, 95% prediction interval | 20.1 |
| **Curve**, in a market with its own data, p10 to p90 | -12.9% to -11.9% a year | `latvia_time_report.md`, 52 monthly refits | 2.4 |
| **Curve**, in a market with no history, 80% prediction interval | -11.7% to -6.6% a year | `drivers_report.md` interval, narrowed from 95% to 80% | 12.6 |
| **Level**, p10 to p90 | -10.0% to +25.3% over 3 years | this report, 26 EU markets | 26.5 |
| **Level**, worst window observed | -27.0% over 3 years | this report | 20.2 |

- **In a market the group already sells in, level risk is about 8 times curve risk.** The curve is known to within about 3.5 points of list price; the market level moves by about 26 points across an ordinary three-year window.
- **In a market or a brand with no history, the two are comparable** (20 points against 26, a ratio of 1.3). That is the thin-slice case, and it is the one pooling fixes: `lodo_report.md` shows borrowing pays only while a slice has few cars of its own.
- **Like for like, the gap is wider still.** The level row is a p10-p90 band; the first two curve rows are the full range of 52 refits and a 95% interval, both wider measures. At p10-p90 on both sides, level risk is about 11 times curve risk in a known market (2.4 points) and 2.1 times in a market with no history (12.6 points). The headline comparison is therefore the conservative one. The value-at-risk model uses the p10-p90 rows, so its curve downside sits at the same one-in-ten as its level shock.
- **The two errors therefore need two different remedies.** Curve error is a modelling problem and a shared value engine solves it. Level error is not a modelling problem at all: no amount of car data forecasts it, so it has to be priced into the decision and re-marked as it moves.

### The same over one year

Most of the group's buy-back payables fall due within a year ([Form 20-F FY2025, Note 24](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1605484/000160548426000021/stellantis-20251231.htm)), so the value-at-risk model also needs the one-year version. A car that keeps 90.8% of its list price after one year at the pooled 9.2%-a-year rate is the one-year reference point. Same measured ranges as above, over one year, and the level from 12-month moves across all 26 markets:

| source of error | points of list price at 1y |
|---|---|
| **Curve**, in a market with its own data, p10 to p90 | 1.0 |
| **Curve**, in a market with no history, 80% prediction interval | 5.1 |
| **Level**, p10 to p90 of 12-month moves | 14.0 |

Over one year level risk is about 13 times curve risk in a known market and 2.8 times in a market with no history.

## What this means for the case

1. **A residual value set today is a bet on the market, not on the car.** The car part is knowable to a few points. The market part is not knowable at all at a three-year horizon, and has actually moved -27% over three years in this sample.
2. **So the ledger's job is not a better forecast.** It is to separate the two, price the part that cannot be forecast, and re-mark it monthly - the design rule `latvia_time_report.md` arrived at from the other direction.
3. **And the decisions that stay open are worth more than the forecast.** A contractual residual is locked on day one; when to offer the upgrade, which channel to sell through and which country to sell in are not. Those are where a moving level can be answered.

## Limits

- **These are official consumer price indices, not the group's own realised prices.** Each statistical office quality-adjusts its own way, and the basket is consumer second-hand purchases, not a lease book's mix.
- **The windows overlap**, so they are not independent draws. The percentiles describe the range of history, not a sampling distribution.
- **Official indices may understate the moves.** `latvia_time_report.md` found our advert-based Latvian index rose +42% against Eurostat's +8.8% over the same months, with Latvia's neighbours (LT +38%, EE +38%) sitting with our figure. If that pattern holds elsewhere, the level risk here is a floor, not a ceiling.
- **The common window is 2016-12 to 2025-12**, which contains the 2021-22 shortage and its unwinding. That is a real episode, not an outlier to be removed, but a different decade would give different percentiles.
- The curve figures compared here come from advert prices; the level indices are built from transaction data. They are not the same kind of measurement.
